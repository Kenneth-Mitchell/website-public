You Can See Everything and the Dereliction of Truth

2026-10-27 · 11 min read

contents

  • The Modality of “Can”
  • You Can See Everything
  • The Dereliction of Truth

Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s You Can See Everything makes an autological promise to its audience — the title, in self-reference to the film itself, promises that we, the audience, can see everything. The film’s description and marketing further invite the expectation of documentary-style investigative truth seeking — Fielder promises to uncover the true story of the weaver of Theranos’ web of lies herself, Elizabeth Holmes. “Who really is Holmes? Why did she lie? What is she feeling days before her eleven-year prison sentence?” These are all questions that we expect Fielder to answer, simply by virtue of the film’s premise itself. After all, we see a documentary to learn more about its subject: to come out of this film understanding less, not more, would surely be a failure of the documentary, Fielder, and Holmes herself.

And yet, after sitting through its nearly three-hour runtime, I left the theatre into the dead of night, finding myself asking more questions than I had come in with. I was prepared to question Holmes’ story — though found myself less prepared to question the film through which I had been watching her.

The Modality of “Can”

There is an extended scene in which Fielder interrogates Holmes on what she meant by “The device can run any test.” This statement is prototypical of Theranos’ downfall — a sweeping promise whose apparent simplicity shrouds a gap between the company’s claim and what is actually true. Holmes, however, maintains that the statement was true. The device was capable of running any test, provided that the tests were developed in the first place. Fielder finds this difficult to accept: what does it mean to say that a device can do something if it actually cannot?

Holmes could mean at least three things by this statement:

  1. The device can run any test with its existing technology
  2. The device could run any test after further development
  3. It is possible in principle to build a device that can run any test

These statements say progressively less about the world we actually inhabit: (1) describes an existing capability, (2) depends on the success of future work, and (3) need not establish anything about Theranos’ device at all. To defend the third is hardly a vindication of someone who led others to believe the first.

“Can” concerns possibility, but possibility under what conditions? Which facts about the world are we allowed to change such that the statement would become true? If we permit the hypothetical technological advancements that Theranos had yet to achieve, we might imagine its device doing everything that Holmes promised. But imagining these advancements does not sufficiently establish that the device possessed the capability, simply that the capability existed in theory. The defense that Holmes offers moves her claim to a world in which the required advancements had already been achieved, while simultaneously asking us to accept that she is still accurately describing our world.

Fielder tries to understand this discrepancy through Abbott and Costello’s “Who’s on First?” This comedy routine plays with the misunderstandings between a speaker and a listener: one man is talking about the first baseman, named “Who,” while the other hears the question “Who?”, and neither can work out why the other isn’t making sense. Upon viewing, Holmes apparently does not understand why the routine is funny, nor why Fielder thinks it bears on their discussion at all. Holmes’ partner Evans interjects here, arguing that her brain does not work that way. He claims that Holmes does not understand language in the ways that we do, using her misunderstanding as a defense against the responsibility for what her words led others to believe. Evans effectively shifts a debate about what a device can do to a debate about what Holmes can comprehend.

Evans’ defense carries a strange implication: she should be believed when she says she didn’t lie because, according to him, she does not understand language as others do. But accepting his defense is also accepting his account of her mind. He speaks on her behalf to explain why her words should not carry the responsibility which Fielder assigns them.

Fielder’s challenge makes sense. A founder cannot make claims about her product, then insist that her private understanding of her claims solely determines what those claims actually meant. But as he and Evans argue, Holmes’ role in the conversation diminishes and she begins to cry while the men on either side of her discuss her own autonomy. Her difficulty with the joke becomes evidence for the men to analyze — their interpretations of which determine how everything else she says should be received. Evans has positioned himself with authority over Holmes’ reality and thus anything she says, even a protest, could be further interpreted as her lack of understanding. What room does Evans’ defense leave her to explain herself, or even disagree with him?

You Can See Everything

Holmes’ relationship to language is further called into question when she discusses one-on-one with Fielder the nature of the documentary itself. Fielder asks if they will actually let the crew go behind every door and film every secret — to which Holmes responds emphatically yes. It is revealed that “You can see everything” is actually a quotation from Holmes herself. The earlier dispute over “can” makes her claim difficult to accept. What conditions is this promise predicated on, and are they ones that Fielder, or we, are aware of?

Holmes and Evans make an effort to demonstrate their openness. We are invited into their home, among their young children; we watch them speak with lawyers and investors, make breakfast, and appear naked. Their blasé attitude towards Fielder and his crew begs the unspoken question: “What more could we have to hide?” They implicitly offer that their extreme exposure and openness is sufficient to establish their honesty writ large.

And yet, we certainly cannot see everything. Evans repeatedly shuts down interviews with Holmes and her parents, encourages Holmes to hide her laptop screen, and literally shuts the door on the camera. Again, Evans seems to claim authority over what Holmes’ language means, thereby exonerating her of the responsibility of her words. Put another way, Evans is an asshole so Holmes appears innocent by comparison.

By using Holmes’ quote as the film’s title, the filmmakers extend its promise to us, the audience. Where Holmes’ “you” referred to Fielder and his crew, the film’s “you” appears to be us. We nevertheless understand that what Fielder is permitted to see is greater than what we, the audience, can. There are moments where the audio is cut, or filming is not allowed, leaving us, the audience, in the dark.

We return, then, to scrutinizing the modality of “can.” Is Holmes’ meaning of “can” predicated on Evans’ exclusion of our access? Does Holmes mean that Fielder is capable of seeing everything, or that he will? And how does that promise extend to us, the audience? Holmes and Evans again hide behind the obfuscation of their language — who is “you”, what does “can” mean, and what really is “everything”?

The Dereliction of Truth

Suppose we accept these qualifications: that the device “can” run any test, provided that the necessary technology were developed; that we “can” see everything, provided that it excludes whatever they choose to conceal. Even if we treat these as truthful, what have these claims established? Certainly less than the statements originally appeared to promise. The defensibility of these statements is granted at the expense of their original meaning. Such a defense is not very useful at all.

Holmes’ defenses approach tautology: these statements are true by their logical form, regardless of the happenstance of the world. For instance, “It is either raining or not raining” is certainly true, but tells us nothing about the weather. Statements are epistemologically useful when they could turn out to be false; for instance “It is raining if the day is Tuesday” tells us a lot more about the weather. If Tuesday arrives without rain, the statement is false. Holmes’ qualified claims are conditionals too, but of a stranger sort. Tuesday has nothing to do with the rain, and anyone can check a calendar. “Provided that the tests were developed” is just another way of saying “provided that the device could run them.” Certainly, the device can run any test, provided that it can run any test. Her condition cannot fail, so neither can her claim. The defense of Holmes’ language pushes it toward tautological truth, its meaning hiding behind a shroud of endless tautology. A room of mad men raving tautologies at each other gains no novel information. Preserving the truth of a statement is not sufficient for that statement communicating anything of substance.

After all, words are spoken for reasons. They ask us to believe things about a world that we share, and often inform us how to act on those beliefs. “Can” does not, by itself, specify which conditions we are supposed to hold fixed — the circumstances by which “can” was spoken serve to establish what is being claimed. We may naturally understand “can” differently when it comes from a founder describing her product to investors, than when it comes from an inventor speculating about a future discovery. But Holmes is both of these. The question is which context is appropriate, and whose responsibility it is to decide that — Holmes or the listener?

Neither Holmes nor the listener can settle this alone. Her intention matters, but the listener must infer it from the information that Holmes makes available. Likewise, a listener’s interpretation must be appropriate to the circumstances of the exchange. What words mean is established through an interaction of these terms — intention and interpretation — and neither is sufficient to establish it on its own.

This does not, however, mean that responsibility is evenly distributed between the speaker and listener. If Holmes intends that her claim is dependent on future development, she should make that condition explicit. The listener cannot recover an unstated condition simply via careful interpretation. Furthermore, by presenting her statement as a description of her product’s capabilities, Holmes gives her audience reason to assume that she is communicating something informative (non-tautological). A rational listener need not entertain every interpretation under which her words might be true, especially those which establish practically nothing about the device. Her defense therefore cannot simply transfer responsibility to the listener. Holmes benefits from the perceived capabilities of her device (through financial gains), but then attempts to walk back the capabilities of her device when challenged. Holmes is attempting to relinquish the responsibility of what her words mean after having benefitted from the obfuscation that she created.

Documentaries incur similar responsibilities. By virtue of the art form, an audience comes to a documentary to learn about its subject. We presuppose that the filmmaker’s presentation will establish understanding about the subject, just as we presuppose that a founder’s description of their product establishes its capabilities. This is not to say that documentaries are unbiased — that is obviously not the case. However, even when a documentary adopts an explicit point of view, we expect that point of view to be sufficient for establishing truths. This is further imposed by the film’s title, extending a faulty statement from Holmes to us, the audience. And yet, this film certainly does not show us everything. Nor does it show the things one might reasonably expect in a documentary about Elizabeth Holmes, such as details on the story of Theranos. The title is the film’s own version of “the device can run any test”: a claim whose conditions are unstated, made to an audience with every reason to take it at face value.

The film further complicates this dynamic by making Fielder himself available for scrutiny. We observe Fielder watching Holmes, and thus our interpretation of Holmes is filtered through a lens of Fielder’s reactions and lines of questioning. This film is less a documentary about Holmes, and more a documentary about Fielder making a documentary about Holmes. Fielder, much like Holmes, leaves this obfuscated, as he reenacts, retells, edits, disagrees with others and changes his mind on events.

This dereliction of truth from Fielder becomes more apparent as the film continues, and we lose access to Holmes, to footage, to audio — relying more heavily on Fielder’s account of what happened, an account which is ultimately challenged by others in the film. Near the end, when Fielder is challenged about whether he himself is hiding something, he has little to say in response. He has spent the last three hours compelling Holmes to take responsibility for what her words meant, but when he is asked to do the same regarding his accounts, he falls silent. Fielder relinquishes the responsibility of telling the audience what is true in much the way that Holmes does. It would be easy to say that Fielder has simply become like Holmes. I think it is rather that the truth, twisted and scrutinized to this degree, starts to look a lot like Holmes’ version of it: true under conditions that nobody bothered to predicate.

I stated earlier that leaving a documentary understanding less would be a failure. By that criterion, the film fails. But it fails in a way that challenges this very criterion: I walked in expecting to see everything, predicated on conditions I never thought to ask about. Were my presuppositions about the film reasonable? Am I, the listener, to blame for my interpretation — or is it Fielder and Holmes who are to blame, for their presentation? The film closes with Fielder joining Holmes in the ocean, both literally and figuratively, jumping into the murky water alongside her.
